Learning the no-trade band

We sell an option and must hedge it by trading the stock every day. Each trade costs money, so the question is when to trade and how much. We solve this control problem three ways: a published formula, exact dynamic programming, and a neural policy trained by differentiating through the P&L.

With 10 bps trading costs, the learned no-trade band is within 0.5% of the exact optimum on unseen paths. The published Whalley–Wilmott band is no better than plain daily delta hedging at that cost.

The control problem

One option, 21 trading days, a trade every day

On day 0 we sell a one-month at-the-money call on one share and collect its premium. Every day the policy chooses how many shares to hold. At expiry we pay the option's payoff and sell the shares. Holding the Black–Scholes delta cancels small price moves, but delta changes every day, and every trade pays a cost proportional to the dollars traded.

Without costs, each day can be decided on its own: hold delta. With costs, today's position is tomorrow's starting point, so a trade now changes what every later trade costs. The best policy is then a no-trade band around delta: hold your position while it is inside the band, and otherwise trade only to its nearest edge. How wide the band should be depends on the whole remaining horizon.

Disturbance wtThe market's next move: price St+1/St and pricing volatility σ̂t+1. From a simulator or replayed history.
State xtSpot St, volatility σ̂t, shares held qt, cash ct, days left.
Control utShares to hold until tomorrow. Constraint: 0 ≤ ut ≤ 1.
Dynamicsqt+1 = ut; cash pays (ut − qt)St plus cost λ|ut − qt|St, then earns interest.
ObjectiveMinimize a risk measure of final P&L = premium + share gains − payoff − costs: entropic risk (1/a)·log E[e−a·P&L] in the Black–Scholes world, CVaR 5% on real data.
InformationThe policy sees only what is known on day t: no look-ahead. Learned policies are trained on data before the test period.
AssumptionWe are a price taker: our trades do not move the market, so history can be replayed as the disturbance. This also makes the P&L differentiable in our actions.
BaselinesDaily delta hedging, a fixed no-trade band, and the Whalley–Wilmott (1997) band, the published asymptotically optimal rule under proportional costs.

Why not plain RL?

Differentiate through the P&L instead of guessing

Policy-gradient methods such as REINFORCE and PPO estimate the gradient from which random actions happened to come with good outcomes (the score function). Because our trades do not move prices, the P&L is a differentiable function of the positions, so we can backpropagate through the whole episode instead (the pathwise gradient). Both are unbiased estimates of the same gradient, computed here on the same episodes and noise.

Limitations

What this does not show

  • Synthetic options. Real data gives real price paths, but option prices come from Black–Scholes at VIX. Real quotes would add a smile, a bid-ask spread on the option, and different vegas.
  • Price taker. Our trades never move prices. That is reasonable for one option on SPY, not for a large book.
  • Thin tails in the test period. The SPY result rests on two crashes. It is consistent across seeds and training sources, but the confidence intervals are wide.
  • The ground truth needs the model. Exact DP is only available in the Black–Scholes world with entropic risk; on real data we have baselines but no optimum.
  • One hedging instrument. We hedge with the stock only. Hedging volatility risk would need a second option and a vector action.
