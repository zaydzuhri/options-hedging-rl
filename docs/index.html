IFT 6162 · Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Control · Fall 2026

Plan or learn the no-trade band?

We sell an option and hedge it by trading the stock every day. Each trade costs money, so the question is when to trade and how much. We find the trading policy two ways and check both against the exact answer: plan it online with model predictive control, which needs a model of the market but no training, or learn it offline with deep hedging, which needs data but no model.

With 10 bps trading costs, MPC closes 93% of the gap between daily delta hedging and the exact optimum on unseen paths, without any training; the learned band closes 96%, and the published Whalley–Wilmott band none of it. On four real stocks in 2019–2025, the planners gave the steadiest gains over daily delta, while the learned policies ranged from the best to the worst, depending on the trend they had learned.

The control problem

One option, 21 trading days, a trade every day

On day 0 we sell a one-month at-the-money call on one share and collect its premium. Every day the policy chooses how many shares to hold. At expiry we pay the option's payoff and sell the shares. Holding the Black–Scholes delta cancels small price moves, but delta changes every day, and every trade pays a cost proportional to the dollars traded.

Without costs, each day can be decided on its own: hold delta. With costs, today's position is tomorrow's starting point, so a trade now changes what every later trade costs. The best policy is then a no-trade band around delta: hold your position while it is inside the band, and otherwise trade only to its nearest edge. How wide the band should be depends on the whole remaining horizon.

Disturbance wtThe market's next move: price St+1/St and pricing volatility σ̂t+1. From a simulator or replayed history.
State xtSpot St, volatility σ̂t, shares held qt, cash ct, days left.
Control utShares to hold until tomorrow. Constraint: 0 ≤ ut ≤ 1.
Dynamicsqt+1 = ut; cash pays (ut − qt)St plus cost λ|ut − qt|St, then earns interest.
ObjectiveEvery method minimizes the same thing: the entropic risk (1/a)·log E[e−a·P&L] of the final P&L = premium + share gains − payoff − costs. It equals minus the certainty equivalent, the sure amount a risk-averse seller would trade the random P&L for. a = 1 in the Black–Scholes world, a = 0.3 on real data.
InformationThe policy sees only what is known on day t: no look-ahead. Everything is fit, trained or tuned on data before the test period.
AssumptionWe are a price taker: our trades do not move the market, so history can be replayed as the disturbance. This also makes the P&L differentiable in our actions.

Methods

Four ways to choose the trade

All four answer the same question, how many shares to hold until tomorrow, for the same objective. They differ in what they need: a formula, a model, or data. Daily delta hedging, which ignores costs, is the baseline.

Whalley–Wilmott band

Needs: a formula

The published answer for small costs and continuous trading (1997): hold anything within (3/2 · e−rτ · λ · S · Γ² / a)1/3 of delta. The band is wide where gamma is large, because there delta jumps around and chasing it is expensive.

Dynamic programming

Needs: the model, a small state

Backward induction over (day, spot, shares held), from expiry to today. With entropic risk the cash drops out of the value function, so the state stays three-dimensional and the answer is exact: the true optimum when the model is right, our ground truth.

Model predictive control

Needs: a model to simulate · no training

Every day: simulate 1,024 futures from the model, choose today's trade and a no-trade band for the remaining days (three numbers) by gradient descent through the simulated P&L, make today's trade, and plan again tomorrow from what actually happened. About 10 ms per decision.

Deep hedging

Needs: data or a simulator · training

A small network outputs the edges of a no-trade band from moneyness, time and volatility. It is trained offline by backpropagating the risk of the P&L through whole episodes; each decision is then one forward pass.

Where to stop trading

In a simulated Black–Scholes market (σ = 20%, no drift) the model is known, so dynamic programming gives the exact optimum to compare against. Real data · interactive

Four real stocks, 2019–2025, never seen in training

Real daily prices of SPY, Coca-Cola, Johnson & Johnson and Apple. The options are synthetic (Yahoo has no historical option prices): SPY options are priced at VIX, the stocks at their trailing 21-day realized volatility. The test set is a one-month call started every five trading days from 2019 to 2025, about 350 per stock. Nothing is fit, trained or tuned on it.

The planners (DP and MPC) plan with a plain Black–Scholes model at the pricing volatility, with the interest rate as the drift: they do not know the real dynamics. The learned policies train on each stock's history before 2019 (from 2005 for SPY, 1990 for the others), or on a GJR-GARCH model fitted to it. Everyone minimizes entropic risk with a = 0.3 (with a = 1, a single bad month would decide the score).

Why not plain RL?

Differentiate through the P&L instead of guessing

Both MPC and deep hedging improve a policy by differentiating the simulated P&L: our trades do not move prices, so the P&L is a differentiable function of the positions (the pathwise gradient). Policy-gradient methods such as REINFORCE and PPO instead estimate the gradient from which random actions happened to come with good outcomes (the score function). Both are unbiased estimates of the same gradient, computed here on the same episodes and noise.

Limitations

What this does not show

  • Synthetic options. Real data gives real price paths, but option prices come from Black–Scholes at VIX or realized volatility. Real quotes would add a smile, a bid-ask spread on the option, and different vegas.
  • A simple model for the planners. On real data DP and MPC plan with Black–Scholes at the pricing volatility. MPC can plan with any simulator (it already does GJR-GARCH); a better market model is the obvious next step.
  • Compute per decision. MPC solves an optimization every day (about 10 ms for 1,024 scenarios); a trained network answers in microseconds. Training is the learned policy's up-front cost.
  • Price taker. Our trades never move prices. That is reasonable for one option on SPY, not for a large book.
  • Thin tails in the test period. The worst test months come from a few events (early 2020, March 2025), so the real-data intervals are wide.
  • The ground truth needs the model. The exact optimum is only available in the Black–Scholes world; on real data we can compare methods, not measure their distance to the best.
